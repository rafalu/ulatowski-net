DAY 1

Monday — Bari

Vecchia + Murat · arrival ~10:00 · no train needed

You land around 10:00. No trains today. Drop the bags, settle in, and get to know the city on foot. Bari Vecchia is one of Italy’s most atmospheric medieval quarters — pasta-making nonnas in the street, a Norman castle, and Romanesque basilicas.

🏛️

What’s worth seeing in Bari

curated, not a route
  • Basilica di San Nicola — Apulian Romanesque (1087–1197), the city’s spiritual core. Crypt holds the relics of Saint Nicholas (yes, the original Santa Claus, stolen from Myra by Bari sailors in 1087). One of the very few places in Italy where Catholic and Orthodox rites are celebrated at the same altar — Orthodox liturgy in the crypt on Thursday mornings.
  • Cattedrale di San Sabino — 12th-c. cathedral rebuilt after the 1156 Norman destruction. The Succorpo below the floor is an underground archaeological route through a paleochristian basilica, Byzantine mosaics and a stretch of Roman road. Twice a year around the solstice the rose-window light projects perfectly onto the floor mosaic — the so-called “sun miracle”.
  • Castello Normanno-Svevo — started by Roger II of Sicily (1132), rebuilt by Frederick II Hohenstaufen, later Aragonese. Inside lives the Gipsoteca, a collection of plaster casts of the best Apulian Romanesque sculpture from across Puglia.
  • Strada delle Orecchiette (Via Arco Basso / Arco Alto) — Bari’s signature street scene: the “nonne delle orecchiette” shaping pasta by hand on wooden boards in the alley. Mornings only (best before 11:00), free, no theatre — it’s actually their work.
  • Piazza Mercantile + Colonna Infame — the medieval merchants’ square with the Colonna della Giustizia, the column to which insolvent debtors were tied to be publicly shamed.
  • Muraglia — the elevated old-town wall promenade. The most underrated view of Bari Vecchia: rooftops on one side, the port on the other.
  • Teatro Petruzzelli — Italy’s fourth-largest opera house, destroyed by arson in 1991 and reopened in 2009. Guided backstage tour if no rehearsal is on.
  • Teatro Margherita — 1914 Liberty-style theatre built on stilts over the port water, today a contemporary-art space. An architectural oddity you don’t expect in Bari.
  • Lungomare Nazario Sauro — one of Italy’s longest urban seafronts, laid out in the 1920s–30s. The Fascist-era razionalismo palazzi between the sea and the Murat grid are Bari’s 20th-c. face.
  • Pinacoteca Metropolitana “Corrado Giaquinto” — regional gallery on the Lungomare with Bellini, Tintoretto, Veronese and Apulian Baroque painters. Calm, well-lit, almost empty on weekdays.
  • Fortino di Sant’Antonio Abate — seaward bastion where the old walls meet the Adriatic. The pure-sunset balcony of Bari.

Pick what actually appeals — this is a menu of context, not a checklist to tick.

DAY 2

Tuesday — Polignano a Mare

🚆 Trenitalia 25 min · €3.20 · cliffs, old town, Lama Monachile

The iconic cliffs of southern Italy. Go early before the day-trip buses arrive. A whitewashed old town hanging above the Adriatic, and the birthplace of Domenico Modugno (“Volare”).

🏛️

What’s worth seeing in Polignano a Mare

curated, not a route
  • Lama Monachile (Cala Porto) — the postcard cove squeezed inside a karst gorge. The Roman bridge above carries the ancient Via Traiana, Hadrian’s road from Beneventum to Brindisi. The most photographed spot in Puglia, and earned.
  • Centro storico on the cliff — whitewashed old town on a 20-metre limestone wall. Wander it slowly; the light at golden hour does the work for you.
  • Statua di Domenico Modugno — life-size bronze of the local-born singer of “Volare”, arms thrown open toward the sea on Largo Ardito. Embarrassingly photogenic at sunset.
  • Terrazza Santo Stefano — the “official” clifftop viewpoint over the open Adriatic. Directly below sits the Grotta Palazzese cave-restaurant — skip eating there (overpriced, mediocre reviews), peek from above and move on.
  • The three medieval gates — Arco Marchesale (grand entrance), Porta Vecchia, Porta Piccola. Each one is a layer of Polignano’s defensive history.
  • The murales of Guido il Genovese — poetic Italian one-liners painted on doors, walls and steps across the old town by Guido Lombardi. Polignano’s most-Instagrammed detail after the cove. Quietly turned into the town’s second identity.
  • Chiesa Matrice (Santa Maria Assunta) — 13th-c. parish church on Piazza Vittorio Emanuele, with works by Renaissance sculptor Stefano da Putignano.
  • Palazzo dell’Orologio — the clock-tower palazzo (former Palazzo del Governatore) closing the main square. The Polignano composition you’ve seen on every guidebook cover.
  • Grotte di Polignano (sea caves) — a network of caves along the coast: Palazzese, delle Rondinelle, Ardito, dei Colombi. Only properly visible from the water — small-boat tour 45–60 min from the harbor.
  • Abbazia di San Vito — 10–11th-c. Benedictine abbey on a tiny harbor 3 km north, with painted votive walls. Quiet, off the day-tripper circuit.
  • Fondazione Pino Pascali — contemporary-art museum dedicated to Polignano-born sculptor Pino Pascali, set in a former 19th-c. slaughterhouse with sea views. The non-obvious cultural stop.

The old town is small — every sight above is inside a 700 m radius. Wander, don’t march.

DAY 3

Wednesday — Monopoli

🚆 Trenitalia 35–45 min · €3.80 · harbor, castle, beach within the walls

The older, calmer cousin of Polignano. A real fishing harbor with painted gozzi boats, an Aragonese castle by the sea, fewer Instagram crowds, and better food for the money. The town locals would quietly recommend instead.

🏛️

What’s worth seeing in Monopoli

curated, not a route
  • Castello Carlo V — pentagonal Spanish coastal fortress built in 1552 on a Norman base, lodged into the harbor. Now a venue for rotating exhibitions; the ramparts give the best view back over the old town.
  • Cattedrale Maria SS. della Madia — late-Baroque cathedral rebuilt 1693–1772, with a 60-metre bell tower. Its icon of the Madonna della Madia arrived (according to the 1117 legend) by sea on a raft of timber beams — exactly the beams the unfinished church was missing. Re-enacted twice a year.
  • Porto Vecchio — still a working fishing harbor, with the traditional painted gozzi wooden boats lined up against the walls. Sunset here is the real Monopoli moment, no filter required.
  • Cala Porta Vecchia — a tiny sand beach tucked literally inside the city walls. The angle that makes Monopoli look like a town built on the sea.
  • Centro storico — labyrinth of whitewashed alleys, baroque palazzi and miniature churches. Far less polished than Polignano’s old town — which is the whole point.
  • Chiesa di Santa Maria Amalfitana — 12th-c. Romanesque-Byzantine church built by Amalfitan merchants over an underground cave grotto. Enter the upper church, descend into the cave church below. Unique.
  • Palazzo Palmieri — 18th-c. noble palace on Piazza Palmieri, the most elegant square of the centro storico.
  • Bastione Santa Maria — defensive bastion at the seaward edge of the old town, today a panoramic walk above the rocks.
  • Diocesan Museum + Crypt of the Cathedral — Byzantine icons, gold liturgical objects, and an unusual wall of ex-voto marinari — votive paintings sailors offered after surviving a shipwreck.
  • Capitolo & Lido Pantano (south coast) — the long stretch of dune beaches and rocky coves that make Monopoli a real beach town. Reach by e-bike or local bus. In May, more for a walk than a swim.
  • Abbazia di Santo Stefano — 12th-c. Benedictine abbey on a cliff 4 km north of town, often used as a wedding venue. Quiet detour with sea views.

Monopoli rewards slow walks more than monument-hunting. Castle + harbor + one cave church is plenty.

DAY 4

Thursday — Trani

🚆 Trenitalia 35–45 min · ~€4 · cathedral by the sea, Giudecca, Moscato di Trani

The underrated star. An 11th-century Romanesque cathedral built right by the sea — one of the most beautiful churches in Italy, and much less crowded than Polignano. Fishing harbor, Jewish quarter, and a different coastline north of Bari. The change of direction keeps the trip fresh.

🏛️

What’s worth seeing in Trani

curated, not a route
  • Cattedrale di San Nicola Pellegrino — the photo-icon of Puglia. Pure Apulian Romanesque (1099–1186), built directly on the rocky shore with no other buildings around it — almost unique in Italy. Three stacked levels below the upper church: the crypt of San Nicola Pellegrino (the 19-year-old Greek pilgrim who died in Trani in 1094), the older church of Santa Maria della Scala, and at the very bottom the paleochristian hypogeum of San Leucio. Bronze doors by Barisano da Trani (1175) — a masterpiece of medieval metalwork.
  • Castello Svevo di Trani — Frederick II Hohenstaufen castle (1233–49), with a rare seawater moat on the harbor side. Smaller than Bari’s castle but the setting is unbeatable.
  • Porto di Trani — one of the most active fishing harbors of the southern Adriatic. Catch it at two moments: 07:30 (boats unloading) and 18:30 (aperitivo on the quay). Two completely different cities.
  • Giudecca (Jewish quarter) — Trani held one of the most important Jewish communities in medieval southern Italy. Wander Via La Giudea and the surrounding alleys; the layout has barely changed since the 13th century.
  • Sinagoga Scolanova — one of the oldest synagogues in Europe still in religious use. Built late 13th c., converted to the church of Santa Maria di Scolanova after the 1492 expulsion, reconsecrated as a synagogue in 2006.
  • Chiesa di Sant’Anna (ex-Scola Grande) — the former main synagogue, with the original Hebrew dedication stone (1247) still embedded in the wall. Today the seat of the Jewish Museum.
  • Villa Comunale — the public park on the rocky promontory beside the cathedral. Pines, sea on three sides, benches. One of the most romantic 20-minute walks of the whole trip at sunset.
  • Chiesa di Ognissanti — small 12th-c. Romanesque church founded by the Templars as a hospital for crusaders embarking from Trani’s port. Easy to miss, worth the detour.
  • Palazzo Caccetta — rare 15th-c. example of Catalan-Gothic civil architecture in southern Italy. Look up at the windows — the carving is the giveaway.
  • Polo Museale di Palazzo Beltrani / Pinacoteca Ivo Scaringi — 19th-c. palazzo with a small picture gallery focused on Trani-born painters. The unexpected cultural stop on a rainy hour.
  • Moscato di Trani DOC — the sweet golden wine from the local Moscato Reale grape, made nowhere else with the same character. Drink it as aperitif at sunset, not as dessert — a Trani-only detail most visitors miss.

Cathedral + castle + harbor + Giudecca = the four-stop spine. Everything else is bonus, depending on time and weather.

DAY 4 · ALT

Thursday — Matera

🚆 FAL ~1h45–2h · Sassi, caves, sunset, wine · romantic stretch day

⚠️ Alternative to Trani. Use only if you accept longer transit (~1h45–2h each way via FAL) and higher walking intensity. Trani is the safer default. Check FAL timetable the day before — current disruption on Matera Sud–Altamura with bus replacement (valid from Dec 2025 "until further notice"). No FAL service on Sundays/holidays.

The most dramatic day of the trip. Matera’s Sassi — UNESCO cave houses, churches, monasteries built into natural caves — are cinematic, ancient, and genuinely romantic at sunset. It breaks the 1-hour rule, but if you want one unforgettable romantic day, this is it.

🏛️

What’s worth seeing in Matera

curated, not a route
  • The two Sassi — Caveoso & Barisano (UNESCO, 1993) — the two cave neighborhoods carved into the gravine. Sasso Caveoso is the rougher, more cave-like one; Sasso Barisano has more baroque facades layered over the rock. Inhabited continuously for ~9,000 years — one of the oldest occupied settlements on Earth.
  • Civita & Cattedrale della Madonna della Bruna — the rocky spur between the two Sassi, the original urban core, crowned by the 1230–70 Apulian Romanesque cathedral with a 52 m bell tower.
  • Casa Grotta nei Sassi (Vico Solitario) — a furnished cave-house museum showing how a peasant family lived with their mule and chickens inside the cave until the 1952 forced evacuation — when De Gasperi’s government declared the Sassi “a national disgrace”. The single most explanatory €5 of the day.
  • Palombaro Lungo — gigantic underground cistern (~5 million litres) carved under Piazza Vittorio Veneto: 50 m × 15 m × 15 m. Guided visit only, ~€5. The “other Matera” no one expects.
  • Rock churches (Chiese Rupestri) — over 150 in the area, carved into the tufo. Easy from town: Santa Maria de Idris + San Giovanni in Monterrone (linked, same rock spur), Santa Lucia alle Malve (rare 10–11th-c. Byzantine frescoes), Madonna delle Virtù + San Nicola dei Greci (canyon-side complex with rotating contemporary art).
  • MUSMA — Museo della Scultura Contemporanea — set inside the cave Palazzo Pomarici. Carlo Levi, Mirko Basaldella, and 20th-c. Italian sculpture in one of the most original museum spaces in Italy.
  • Belvedere Murgia Timone — across the gravina canyon (15-min descent + climb), the spot used by Mel Gibson to film The Passion of the Christ, by Bond in No Time to Die, and for Wonder Woman 1984. The classic postcard view of the Sassi from outside.
  • Belvedere Luigi Guerricchio (“Tre Archi”) — the easiest in-town viewpoint, at the very entrance to the Sassi. Your first proper photo of Matera.
  • Piazzetta Pascoli — tiny square in front of Palazzo Tramontano; arguably the most beautiful and least crowded viewpoint inside the city.
  • Castello Tramontano — unfinished 16th-c. Aragonese castle on the hill above town. Construction stopped in 1514 when the citizens killed Count Giancarlo Tramontano in a revolt over his taxes. The story alone is worth a stop.
  • Parco della Murgia Materana — the canyon-park on the opposite side of the gravina, with hundreds of rupestrian churches and hermit caves scattered in the cliffs. Trail shoes required, not for this trip unless you skip Matera town.
  • Cripta del Peccato Originale — outside town (~14 km, taxi only), the “Sistine Chapel of cave painting”: 8–9th-c. Lombard frescoes by the “Painter of the Flowers of Matera”. Reservation-only. Skip unless you have a car or extra time.

Matera punishes checklist tourists. Pick 3–4 stops max + viewpoints, and leave room to just walk.

1.5 h+ by FSE with a transfer, then a trulli Disneyland full of tour buses. Save it for a deeper Puglia trip.

Lecce / Gallipoli / Salento

1.5–3 hours away. Not realistic for a relaxed public-transport day trip.

Matera as a rushed checklist day

If doing Matera (see Thu alternative), do it for mood: Sassi walk, one cave/rock-church stop, sunset viewpoint, wine, early dinner. Do not stack 8 attractions.

Murgia Timone without planning

The opposite-canyon viewpoint is stunning but sits across the ravine. Do it only with a planned transfer/tour, or skip it and use city viewpoints (Piazzetta Pascoli, Belvedere Guerricchio).

Grotta Palazzese restaurant

€€€€, mixed reviews, you’re paying for the cave. Admire it from above and move on.

Castellana Grotte

Too much transit, fixed tour times, and it eats half a day.

Tourist-menu places near the castle

Generic Lungomare-adjacent food. Walk 200 meters deeper into the old town instead.

The nonna pasta show as lunch

Watch it, yes. Eat beside plastic tables there, no.

Thinking May means beach weather

The Adriatic will likely be 18–20°C. Quick brave dip? Sure. Full beach day? Not really.

Bari Centrale main hall, 6:00–21:00. Slowest option. Use only if machine is broken.
